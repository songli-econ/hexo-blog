The Self-Hacking Manual: Casting Kotoamatsukami on Oneself

(update: an alternative version is now on SSRN Working Paper.)

Disclaimer: The quit‑smoking example is used solely to illustrate the methodology and does not constitute medical advice.

Because I am not Uchiha Shisui, I cannot cast the authentic Kotoamatsukami, i.e. direct belief manipulation, on myself. However, thanks to the rise of Kagaku Ningu (Scientific Ninja Tools), namely the research of Schwartzstein & Sunderam, “Using Models to Persuade” (AER 2021), and Aina’s tailored‑stories framework, I can at least mimic the effect of Kotoamatsukami.

In this post, I make an example by adapting from 4.2 in Aina (2025) tailored stories.

Settings

Suppose I am a smoking addict attempting to quit. When I make this decision, I know that in the next period I am very likely to lose willpower and give in by smoking another cigarette. My prior belief that I have strong willpower with respect to abstaining from smoking is low, say, 0.050.050.05.

Formally, let the state space to be Ω={H,L}\Omega=\{H,L\}Ω={H,L}, where HHH means I have strong willpower to abstain, and LLL means I am weak, with μ0(H)=0.05\mu_{0}(H)=0.05μ0​(H)=0.05.

The sender is the t=0t=0t=0 self, who decides to quit; the receiver is the t=1t=1t=1 self, who must implement the unpleasant task of smoking cessation. There is also a future self at t=2t=2t=2 who enjoys the health benefits of quitting without suffering, whereas the t=1t=1t=1 self endures the temptation without yet enjoying a healthier body (or mind).

The set of signal realisations is S={G,B}S=\{G,B\}S={G,B}. A good signal GGG could be less coughing, feeling more comfortable during my nightly run, or simply feeling less tempted to smoke. A bad signal BBB could be severe cravings, worse coughing, and so on, i.e. the opposites of the examples in GGG.

All of these examples are observable, consistent with the notion of a signal realisation.

The true signal‑generation process is πtrue(G∣H)=πtrue(B∣L)=0.75\pi_{\text{true}}(G\mid H)=\pi_{\text{true}}(B\mid L)=0.75πtrue​(G∣H)=πtrue​(B∣L)=0.75. Even conditional on HHH, I might occasionally still be short of breath while running (e.g. because recovery takes time or air quality is poor). Likewise, conditional on LLL, my lifestyle might improve by chance, leading to unexpectedly smooth recovery, a mere ¨regression¨ to the mean. Thus the process remains stochastic.

A modelling complication arises: the t=0t=0t=0 self knows the true process, whereas the t=1t=1t=1 self does not (the t=2t=2t=2 self is inactive and merely benefits). I gloss over the epistemic justification, call it the privilege of age and a brain deteriorated by years of smoking.

(As you might already noticed, the t=1t=1t=1 me, or a receiver in this framework generally, is NOT fully (Bayesian) rational: the receiver not only unaware of the true model, but doesn´t even have a prior over possible models; the receiver used only a heurestic rule to choose which model to adopt,…)

Aina refers to GGG or BBB as a “signal”; I call each an individual signal realisation. The difference is terminological only.

The action space is A={SM,NSM}A=\{SM,NSM\}A={SM,NSM}, meaning “smoke” or “not smoke”.

Although I have said the lucky inactive t=2t=2t=2 self enjoys the benefits, these are ex‑post gains worth, say, v=120v=120v=120. From an expected‑utility perspective, both t=0t=0t=0 and t=1t=1t=1 selves value the discounted benefits and costs. The cost of suffering at t=1t=1t=1 is c=5c=5c=5. I exhibit present bias (quasi-hyperbolic discounting) (Laibson 1997, eq. (3), pp. 442–443). Specifically, the t=0t=0t=0 discount‑weight vector is (1,βδ,βδ2)(1,\beta\delta,\beta\delta^{2})(1,βδ,βδ2), and the t=1t=1t=1 vector is (1,1,βδ)(1,1,\beta\delta)(1,1,βδ) (or (1,βδ)(1,\beta\delta)(1,βδ) after dropping the bygone first coordinate).

Interpretation: From the t=0t=0t=0 viewpoint, current utility is weighted by 1; the cost ccc at t=1t=1t=1 has weight βδ\beta\deltaβδ, while the benefit vvv at t=2t=2t=2 has weight βδ2\beta\delta^{2}βδ2. Since δ<1\delta<1δ<1, the future benefit is discounted more heavily than the nearer cost, so quitting is attractive only if the expected benefit is large enough.

Similarly, from the anticipated t=1t=1t=1 perspective, the suffering cost has weight 1, whereas the future benefit has weight βδ\beta\deltaβδ. Therefore the t=0t=0t=0 self expects the t=1t=1t=1 self to feel the pain more acutely than the benefit and perhaps relapse.

We leave the fortunate t=2t=2t=2 self aside; he never decides.

The set of feasible vectors of posteriors

When is a vector of posteriors implementable? In this example, two posteriors are required, one for the good signal realization and another for the bad realization, and each must exceed a threshold determined by ccc,vvv,β\betaβ, and δ\deltaδ. In other words, we need a vector of posteriors whose components are each sufficiently high. A similar criterion is also prevalent in the Bayesian persuasion literature, for example, a posterior higher than 12\frac{1}{2}21​ might be needed in a binary setting, like the classical prosecutor juror example, and the martingale property (that their average equals the prior) requires another posterior to be sufficiently low. However, as noted earlier, the martingale property is inapplicable here because different realizations may prompt the receiver to adopt different models, and weighting the posteriors by their respective likelihoods does not guarantee that they average back to the prior. How, then, should we determine whether a vector of posteriors is feasible? Aina´s Thm 1 gives a harmonic mean criterion, characterizing the set of feasible vectors of posteriors. where a feasible vector of posteriors might not only violate Bayes plausibility (in which the weights must come only from the true model) but also violates Bayes consistency (in which the weights can come from different models): the set of feasible vectors of posteriors contains the latter as proper subsets.

My vector of posterios is (μs1(H),μs1(H))(\mu_{s1}(H),\mu_{s1}(H))(μs1​(H),μs1​(H)) and my piror is μ0(H)\mu_{0}(H)μ0​(H). Take μs1(H)\mu_{s1}(H)μs1​(H), then the movements of this posterior is (μs1(H)μ0(H),1−μs1(H)1−μ0(H))(\frac{\mu_{s1}(H)}{\mu_{0}(H)},\frac{1-\mu_{s1}(H)}{1-\mu_{0}(H)})(μ0​(H)μs1​(H)​,1−μ0​(H)1−μs1​(H)​) where the first coordinate relates to state HHH and the second state LLL. So the maximal movement is max⁡{μs1(H)μ0(H),1−μs1(H)1−μ0(H)}\max\{\frac{\mu_{s1}(H)}{\mu_{0}(H)},\frac{1-\mu_{s1}(H)}{1-\mu_{0}(H)}\}max{μ0​(H)μs1​(H)​,1−μ0​(H)1−μs1​(H)​}.
